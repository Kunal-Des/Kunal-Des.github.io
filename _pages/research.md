My current research focuses on observations of massive binary stars. However, since I studied hot subdwarf binaries for my master thesis, I maintain continued interest in them through occasional projects. Below, I present a short summary of the two main themes along with projects within. 


Massive stars
======
Stars with initial masses $$M_{\rm ini}\gtrsim 8\,M_\odot$$ are termed as massive stars, owing to their very eventful lives and often spectacular deaths leaving behind compact objects (neutron stars or black holes). Although much less in number compared to their low mass counterparts, they strongly influence their surroundings by releasing copious amounts of ionizing radiation and nuclear processed material. We now know that essentially all massive stars are born in binary or multiple systems, based on extensive studies targeting massive stars in the Milky Way as well as the Magellanic Clouds. Furthermore, about 70% are likely to interact (e.g. tides, mass transfer, mergers) with their companions during their lifetime. The rising population of merging stellar-mass black holes detected by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA have prompted efforts to understand how they form, with massive binary evolution considered a leading formation channel.

In this general context, massive binary stars have to jump through several evolutionary hoops before they can end up as merging compact objects. While there are extensive theoretical efforts aiming to understand this picture, observational constraints of massive binaries across different evolutionary phases are a non-negotiable necessity. Statistically significant samples of massive binaries in different phases are key to benchmark binary evolution models and constrain uncertain physics (e.g. mass transfer efficiency, supernova natal kicks, etc.). However, our current knowledge of evolved massive binaries is very limited in terms of sample size as well as stellar/orbital parameters. 

My PhD research primarily aims to improve the situation for two of these evolved phases: the WR + OB phase an the BH + OB phase. Because evolved massive binaries are super interesting, I also explore detours motivated by new findings/ideas along the way. Below, I discuss the two phases and detours within.


Wolf-Rayet stars and the WR + OB situation
------
Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars in the most general sense are stars with spectra dominated by emission lines of helium along with metals such as nitrogen, carbon and oxygen. In the context of massive stars, the so-called classical WRs are evolved, stripped cores of stars with initial masses $$M_{\rm ini}\gtrsim 20\,M_\odot$$. They are typically hydrogen-poor due to stripping either by their own stellar winds or a binary companion. They are also thought to be immediate progenitors of stellar-mass black holes.
We now know that essentially all massive stars are born in binary or multiple systems, based on several studies targeting massive stars in the Milky Way as well as the Magellanic Clouds. Furthermore, about 70% are likely to interact (e.g. tides, mass transfer, mergers) with their companions during their lifetime. As a result, binary evolution is expected to play an important role in the formation of WRs. Inversely, understanding binary properties of the WR population can provide key constraints on binary interactions. 
The rising population of merging stellar-mass black holes detected by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA have prompted efforts to understand how they form, with massive binary evolution considered a leading formation channel.
